the research
Not just us. This is a real, studied pattern.

Every project like this claims it's "backed by research." We actually checked: has anyone studied the exact moment a kid's confidence turns into an adult's "let's be realistic"? Turns out, yes — for decades, from a few different angles.

A kid's dream isn't a throwaway — it's a working system

In 1986, psychologists Hazel Markus and Paula Nurius proposed something called possible selves theory: everyone carries mental images of who they might become — a hoped-for self and a feared self — and these images actively shape motivation, not just describe it. It's not just theory, either. Psychologist Daphna Oyserman ran a real six-week school program built around strengthening kids' "possible selves," and the students who went through it showed measurably better academic outcomes afterward. A separate study interviewed 42 adolescents aged 11–13, and found that an imagined future job was one of the most common things they brought up completely unprompted.

Markus & Nurius, 1986, American Psychologist · Oyserman, Bybee & Terry, 2006, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology

Career regret isn't a small thing — it's near the top of the list

Psychologist Neal Roese, together with Amy Summerville, pooled 11 separate studies asking adults what they regret most in life. Across every single one, the same six categories kept surfacing in roughly the same order: education, career, romance, parenting, sense of self, leisure. Career sits right under education — and the two are often tangled together anyway, since it's usually the choice of major or path that quietly locks in the rest. Follow-up lab studies from the same research line found regret hits hardest for exactly the things you can't go back and redo — which is exactly the position a childhood dream ends up in once you're past a certain age.

Roese & Summerville, 2005, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin

The surprising part: letting go might be the healthy move

This is the finding that changed how we're planning our interviews. Psychologists Carsten Wrosch and Jutta Heckhausen studied 122 people aged 20 to 87, asking how they explained their own biggest regrets. Younger adults who blamed themselves — and believed they still had control — actually felt less regret, because blame plus control means "I can still fix this." Older adults showed the opposite: holding onto self-blame for something truly unfixable made it worse, not better. Saying "it wasn't really up to me" was linked to lower distress — a protective, adaptive shift, not denial. So when an older adult tells us "there was nothing I could do," that might be someone who has actively made peace with something, not someone who's fooling themselves.

Wrosch & Heckhausen, 2002, Psychology and Aging

What our own psychologist told us

We asked our own expert contact — a psychologist at SOS Village Armenia — the same questions, and her answer lines up with the academic research above, but goes further. She told us a childhood dream is rarely about the specific job itself. A kid who wants to be a fashion designer might really be chasing a deeper need underneath: to be recognized, to be heard, to be seen. When an adult doesn't reach that childhood dream, they can carry a heavy feeling of having "done life wrong" — unless they go back and identify the real need behind the old dream, and find another way to meet it. In her words, that's what makes the difference between someone who stays stuck and someone whose story turns out well.

She also reframed something we'd assumed was automatically sad: changing your path as an adult. She called it a normal, healthy sign of growth — priorities shift with different life stages, and someone who chose a path at 18 is allowed to outgrow it by 35. The problem isn't changing direction; it's when someone clings to an old goal purely out of guilt over "wasted time," unable to separate who they are from what they once wanted. She traced that guilt straight back to childhood messages like "if I don't finish, I'm not good enough" — beliefs handed down by parents or society, which make simply outgrowing a goal feel like failure.

Her advice for parents, though, was the part that stuck with us: love a kid unconditionally, not for their achievements — so changing a dream never feels like losing their parents' respect. A child raised that way builds what she called "inner freedom" — the ability to build a life on their own terms, instead of running someone else's script.

From our own interview with a psychologist at SOS Village Armenia, translated from Russian.

The gap nobody's filled yet

Nearly all of this research comes out of the US, UK, and similar Western contexts. Nobody's directly compared whether the pressure to "be realistic" hits at the same age, or gets talked about the same way, across different cultures — and that's exactly the angle our team can add, coming from different countries ourselves. None of it captures the actual texture of the moment either: where someone was standing, who said the sentence, what it felt like right then. A survey gives you a percentage.
